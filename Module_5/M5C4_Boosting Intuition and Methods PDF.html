The Situation

Predicting the resale price of a used car from its mileage.

A first, simple model — just guessing the average price for every car — is obviously wrong for most cars: high-mileage cars sell for less, low-mileage ones for more. Rather than throw that simple model away and start over with something more complex, what if you kept it, and trained a second, small model whose only job is to predict how wrong the first one was?

That's the entire idea behind Gradient Boosting: build an ensemble one model at a time, where every new addition specifically targets the current ensemble's mistakes.

The Core Idea

Every new model is trained on the previous model's errors

STEP 1

Start simple

Begin with a basic prediction — often just the average of the target across all training rows.

STEP 2

Measure the error

Compute the residual for every row: actual value minus current prediction.

STEP 3

Fit and add

Train a small new model to predict those residuals, then add its (scaled) prediction to the running total. Repeat.

Each individual model in the sequence can be weak and simple — even a single-split tree. What makes the ensemble strong is that every addition is aimed squarely at whatever the ensemble still gets wrong.

Round 0 is just the average price for every car — badly wrong, MSE 17.23. Each subsequent round fits a simple stump to the current residuals and adds half of its correction (a learning rate of 0.5) to every prediction. Two rounds in, MSE has dropped to 3.74 — less than a quarter of where it started, without ever training a single complicated model.

Why Each Correction Gets Scaled Down

The learning rate controls how much of each fix you actually take

Taking the full correction every round sounds faster, but it means each new model can overreact to whatever noise was in that round's residuals. Scaling every correction down forces the ensemble to improve gradually and lets later rounds correct the small mistakes earlier rounds couldn't. On the same 150-car dataset with 100 boosting rounds, only the learning rate changed:

Rate = 0.01 (too slow)
R² = 0.418
Rate = 0.1 (balanced)
R² = 0.802
Rate = 1.0 (too aggressive)
R² = 0.565

Too slow, and 100 rounds isn't enough to catch up. Too aggressive, and each round overshoots the residual instead of gently narrowing it. A moderate rate, given enough rounds, wins both ends.
