The Situation

A music streaming service has listening data — but no genre labels at all.

For every listener, you know how long, on average, they let a song play before skipping it, and roughly how often they skip. Nobody told the service which listeners prefer which genres — that label simply doesn't exist in the data. And yet, looking at enough listeners, some clearly behave like each other and unlike others. The question this session opens is: can you find that structure without ever being told what to look for?

Defining the Scope

No target to predict — only structure to discover

SUPERVISED LEARNING

Has an answer key

Every training example comes with a known label or value — Late/On-Time, a price, a category — and the model learns to predict it on new examples.

UNSUPERVISED LEARNING

Has no answer key

There is no label to predict at all. The only information available is the data's own features — and the goal is to find whatever structure, grouping, or pattern is already sitting inside it.

This isn't a smaller or easier version of supervised learning — it's a different kind of question entirely: not “what's the right answer for this row,” but “what does this data, as a whole, actually look like?”

The Scope of Unsupervised Learning

Three different questions you can ask unlabeled data

CLUSTERING

“Which rows are alike?”

Group similar data points together — like listeners with similar habits — without predefining what the groups are.

DIMENSIONALITY REDUCTION

“What's the essential shape?”

Compress many features down to the few that actually carry the meaningful variation, for visualization or efficiency.

ANOMALY DETECTION

“What doesn't fit?”

Flag the data points that look nothing like the rest — useful for catching fraud, defects, or sensor faults.

All three start from the same place: no labels, just the data's own features.

Understanding Patterns in Unlabeled Data

Without labels, “similar” has to mean something measurable

If two listeners have nearly identical average song length and skip rate, they're sitting close together in feature space — and that closeness, on its own, is a real signal, even with no label attached to either listener. Distance between data points is the basic building block nearly every unsupervised method relies on: small distance suggests similarity, large distance suggests difference.

distance(A, B) small → A and B likely belong together
Worked Example

8 listeners, plotted by behavior alone — no labels used

Every dot is the same color — nothing here has been labeled or grouped. But the visual clustering is obvious: four listeners sit close together in the lower-left, four sit close together in the upper-right, and the two clusters are far apart. That grouping came entirely from the numbers themselves.

Listeners in the same group sit, on average, over 13 times closer to each other than to listeners in the other group — a pattern discovered without a single label.
